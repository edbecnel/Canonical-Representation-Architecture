We are developing a technology-independent architecture concerned with preserving knowledge while allowing documents, file formats, publications, interfaces, navigation systems, knowledge graphs, search indexes, AI-derived structures, and other representations to evolve.

A foundational term under investigation is:

    Canonical Knowledge

We do not yet have an accepted definition.

Your task is to analyze this term from first principles and propose a precise architectural definition.

Do not assume that canonical knowledge is:

- a document,
- a file,
- a database,
- a knowledge graph,
- a semantic assertion,
- an ontology,
- a representation,
- or any other particular implementation.

Do not assume that the word "canonical" necessarily means true, complete, immutable, or universally authoritative.

Please address the following questions:

1. What should "Canonical Knowledge" mean in a representation-independent architecture?

2. What properties must something possess in order to qualify as canonical knowledge?

3. What properties are commonly associated with canonical knowledge but should not be part of its essential definition?

4. What is the difference between:
   - canonical knowledge,
   - canonical data,
   - canonical documents,
   - authoritative sources,
   - semantic assertions,
   - and derived representations?

5. Can canonical knowledge contain uncertainty, disagreement, competing interpretations, incomplete observations, or historically superseded claims? Explain.

6. Is canonical knowledge necessarily true, or is it instead authoritative within some explicitly defined scope?

7. Does canonical knowledge consist primarily of:
   - meaning,
   - assertions,
   - relationships,
   - concepts,
   - recorded observations,
   - or something more fundamental?

8. What is the minimum information that must survive when all implementation-specific representations are removed?

9. Provide at least five concrete examples of canonical knowledge.

10. Provide at least five concrete counterexamples—things that may appear canonical but are actually representations, indexes, projections, inferences, publications, or other derived artifacts.

11. Invert your proposed definition. Describe a coherent architecture in which representations are primary and canonical knowledge is derived from them. Identify whether that architecture is viable and what consequences follow.

12. Identify any ambiguities or hidden assumptions in the phrase "Canonical Knowledge" itself.

13. Recommend whether the term should be retained, refined, replaced, or divided into multiple more precise concepts.

14. End with:
   - one concise proposed definition,
   - one expanded formal definition,
   - a qualification test that can determine whether an artifact is canonical knowledge,
   - and the strongest objection to your own proposal.

Treat this as an architectural inquiry rather than a philosophical essay or an implementation design.

Do not optimize your answer for agreement with conventional knowledge-management terminology. Challenge the premise where necessary.
